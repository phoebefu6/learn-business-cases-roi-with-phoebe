The assumption doing the work

Every business case has one number on the front page and nine assumptions underneath it, and usually one of the nine is carrying the whole thing. A board that knows which one can ask the right person the right question. A board that only sees the headline argues about the discount rate. Tonight: why estimates lean optimistic and how the outside view corrects them, how to read a tornado in thirty seconds, and how a switching value and a range turn one NPV into a sentence a board can hold. Part 1 · covers optimism bias and reference classes

Every estimate leans the same way

5 min live

Errors in project estimates are not random. If they were, half of all projects would come in under budget. They lean one way: costs higher, durations longer, benefits lower than the plan said. The fix is not a better plan. It is a second view from outside the plan: how did projects like this one actually turn out?

LiveWhy every estimate leans the same way3 min▶

The inside view. A plan is built from the inside: list the tasks, ask each team how long and how much, add it up. Each estimate is honest, and each assumes its task goes roughly as intended. Nobody budgets for the vendor that slips or the integration nobody listed, because nobody can name them yet. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (1979) named this the planning fallacy, and with it the remedy, the outside view; Kahneman and Dan Lovallo (1993) developed the inside view as the thing being corrected.

The outside view. Ignore the details of your plan for a moment and ask how projects like it actually turned out. Bent Flyvbjerg turned that question into a forecasting method, reference class forecasting (Flyvbjerg 2006, Project Management Journal), in three steps: identify a class of comparable past projects, establish the distribution of their outcomes, and place your project in it.

What it does to a case. The inside view gives you a point. The outside view tells you where that point usually lands. If comparable projects overran by a quarter on average, a plan that assumes no overrun is not a base case, it is the best case.

Self-studyUsing your own organisation's history first2 min▶

HM Treasury, The Green Book (2026) says appraisers must account for optimism bias with explicit adjustments at the outset: raise the cost and duration estimates and lower the benefits. It then sets an order of preference. Use your own organisation's historical forecast errors first, because your projects, your vendors and your estimators are the best reference class you will ever have. Fall back on generic values only when you have no history of your own.

The generic values sit in HM Treasury's Supplementary Green Book Guidance on Optimism Bias. For Equipment/Development projects (equipment, ICT, software and systems development) the capital cost upper bound is 200% and the lower bound 10%; the duration upper bound is 54% and the lower bound 10%. The upper bound is a starting point at outline business case stage, to be reduced as the causes of optimism are mitigated. Two honest caveats: the values come from a Mott MacDonald review published in 2002, so the data is old, and the guidance gives no sound bounds for operating costs or benefit shortfalls; for those it points you to sensitivity analysis and switching values, which is Parts 2 and 3.

The 25% uplift on build in tonight's widget is a teaching choice for Keelson, well inside that 200% upper bound, not a figure from the guidance. To build your own reference class, pull the last five to ten projects your organisation approved, and for each put the approved cost next to the actual cost. Your numbers will differ, and they will be more persuasive to your CFO than any national table.

Self-studyWhat the AI failure headlines actually measured3 min▶

Two figures turn up in almost every AI investment debate, and neither is a measured failure rate for AI projects in general. Treat them as contested evidence and know what each one counted.

MIT NANDA, "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" (July 2025, preliminary). The headline is that 95% of organisations get zero return. The body measures something narrower: custom enterprise generative AI tools that reached production with sustained profit-and-loss or productivity impact, checked six months after the pilot. The evidence is 52 interviews, 153 conference surveys and more than 300 public initiatives. The report lists its own limits, selection bias and a short window, and critics call the sample small and non-random and the definition of success narrow.

RAND RR-A2680-1 (August 2024). The often-quoted "more than 80% of AI projects fail" is, as reported, a figure RAND attributes to "by some estimates": an outside estimate RAND cites rather than one it measured.

How to use them in a case. Do not put either number in as your probability of failure. Use them as a prompt to build a real reference class: which of your own organisation's AI pilots reached production, and which still deliver value a year later? If you cannot answer, that gap is itself a finding to put in the case.

Real world

1,471 IT projects. Bent Flyvbjerg and Alexander Budzier, "Why Your IT Project May Be Riskier than You Think", Harvard Business Review, September 2011, studied 1,471 IT projects. The average cost overrun was 27%, which sounds survivable. The finding that matters is the tail: one project in six was a black swan, with an average cost overrun of 200% and a schedule overrun of almost 70%. The sample was mostly public agencies (92%) and US-based (83%), so it is a reference class to argue with, not a law of nature. Its lesson for any board still holds: the average overrun is not the risk; the one-in-six tail is.

Part 2 · covers reading a tornado

Thirty seconds with a tornado

5 min live

A tornado chart answers one question: which assumption moves the answer most? Each input in turn goes to its low and its high while every other input stays at base, and the NPV at each end becomes a bar. Sort the bars longest first and the chart looks like a funnel. You read it from the top, and you usually stop after three.

LiveThirty seconds with a tornado3 min▶

Ten seconds: the top bar. Saving per fast-tracked claim, from -111.5k at its low of 11 to +260.5k at its high of 16. It is the longest bar, and its low end sits furthest below the red line. That is the assumption doing the work.

Ten seconds: which bars cross zero. A bar that crosses the red line means that input alone, at the edge of its range, can make the case lose money. At Keelson four of the nine do: saving per claim, share fast-tracked, leakage per claim and, only just, run cost. The other five stay positive whatever happens to them on their own.

Ten seconds: who owns the top three. Saving per claim, share fast-tracked and leakage per claim are all per-claim economics, and none of them is the data team's number. They belong to the claims operations lead, who knows what a claim costs to handle, how many are genuinely simple and how often an unchecked claim is overpaid. The tornado has just told Maya whose signature the case needs.

Self-studyWhy the argument is usually about the wrong bar2 min▶

Finance teams love to argue about the discount rate: is the hurdle 7%, 9% or 11%? At Keelson that whole argument moves the NPV from +141.7k to +84.1k, and the case stays positive at both ends. It is the shortest bar on the chart. Meanwhile the saving per claim, an operational fact somebody could go and measure, swings it by 372 thousand across its range of 11 to 16. The discount rate gets the airtime because it is the one number finance owns and can debate from a desk.

Two cautions keep the chart honest. First, each bar moves one input while holding the rest at base, so it hides interactions: a low share fast-tracked and a low saving together are worse than either bar shows, and Analyst session 5 handles that by drawing all inputs at once. Second, a bar is only as long as the range somebody gave for it. A narrow range set by the sponsor makes an input look safe. Ask who set each low and high before you trust the order. The analyst's version of this chart, with the full method, is in Analyst session 4.

Real world

Forty minutes on the shortest bar. At the first review of the CFT case, Keelson's finance committee spent most of the meeting on whether the hurdle should be 9% or 11%. Maya put the tornado on the screen and pointed at the bottom bar: at 11% the NPV is still +84.1k. Then she pointed at the top one: if the saving per claim is 11 rather than 14, the NPV is -111.5k. The next question in the room was the first useful one all meeting: who knows what a simple claim really costs us to handle? Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 3 · covers switching values and ranges as the board sentence

The sentence a board can hold

5 min live

A board cannot hold nine bars in its head, and should not have to. It can hold one sentence: the case breaks if this one thing turns out this much worse than we planned, and this person owns that number. Add the range, so nobody mistakes the base case for a promise, and the headline is done.

LiveThe sentence a board can hold3 min▶

The switching value. HM Treasury, The Green Book (2026) defines it as the value a key variable would need to change to for the option to stop being value for money, and asks for it, at a minimum, for the preferred option. In private-sector words: how far can this input move before the NPV hits zero?

Keelson's sentence. "The case breaks if the saving per fast-tracked claim falls 10.7 percent, from 14 to 12.50." Check the percentage by hand: (14 - 12.50) / 14 = 1.50 / 14 = 0.107, so 10.7%. Two other inputs break the case at a 15.0% fall: simple claims per year and the share fast-tracked. All three are within 20% of base, and all three are per-claim economics.

Why it beats the tornado for a board. A tornado shows what happens at the edges of ranges somebody chose. A switching value needs no range at all: it tells the board the distance to the cliff, and leaves the owner of the input to say whether a 10.7% miss is likely. That is a question a claims operations lead can answer from experience.

Self-studyWhy the base case is not the middle2 min▶

Analyst session 5 draws every input at once, 5,000 seeded draws, each from a low, base and high triangle. The result for CFT: P10 -141.5k, P50 +20.5k, P90 +189.9k, and a 44.1% chance that the NPV is below zero. The base case, +111.7k, sits well above the middle draw. That is not a bug. The ranges are skewed against the plan: build cost can rise further than it can fall (330 to 460 around 360), leakage can rise further than it can fall, and the saving per claim is capped close to its base. When most of the surprises available are bad ones, the plan sits near the good end of what can happen.

This is optimism bias seen from the other side. The Green Book's answer is an explicit uplift; Keelson's widget lets you apply a 25% uplift on build, which brings the NPV down to +21.7k, close to the middle draw. The honest headline carries both: the base case, so the reader knows what the plan assumes, and the range, so the reader knows the plan is not the median outcome.

Real world

Maya rewrites the headline. The first board paper said "CFT: NPV +111.7k". Ines asked Maya to rewrite it before it went out. The new version read: "CFT's base case is +111.7k, but the range across 5,000 draws runs from -141.5k (P10) to +189.9k (P90), with a 44.1% chance of loss. The case breaks if the saving per claim falls 10.7 percent, from 14 to 12.50; the claims operations lead owns that number." Tom thought it read like a confession. Ines said it read like a case her team could sign, and that the next question was obvious: can a pilot tell us the saving per claim before we spend the full 360? Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Homework

Before Leader session 4

30 min
  1. Build a small reference class. Find three to five past projects in your organisation like yours. For each, write the approved cost and the actual cost side by side. Your numbers will differ; the direction of the gap is what you are looking for.
  2. Ask the owner of your top assumption. Show the person who owns it the number your case uses and ask one question: what value would surprise you? Their answer is a better low than the one you have.
  3. Write your switching sentence. "The case breaks if ______ falls ____ percent, from ____ to ____." If you cannot work out the percentage, ask whoever built the model; if nobody can, that is the first thing to fix.
  4. Check one AI headline in your own deck. If any slide you own quotes an AI failure rate, find what that study actually measured and rewrite the line to say so.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What did Flyvbjerg and Budzier find across 1,471 IT projects?

The average hides the risk; the one-in-six tail is what sinks a budget. The sample was mostly public agencies and US-based, which is worth saying when you use it.

2 · Which bar tops Keelson's tornado for CFT?

The top three bars are per-claim economics owned by claims operations. The discount rate, the usual argument, is the shortest bar.

3 · What does a switching value tell a board?

It is the distance to the cliff for one input. The owner of that input then says whether a miss that large is likely.
