Time value and the hurdle rate

Last session you built six numbers, one per year. Tonight you find out what they are worth today. Cash that arrives in year 5 is not worth the same as cash in hand, because the money could be earning something else in the meantime. The discount rate is the price of that wait, and choosing it is a judgement about risk and units, not a formality. Tonight: discount factors and present values for Keelson Mutual's claims fast-track triage, the NPV profile that shows where the case breaks, and which rate answers which question. Part 1 · covers discounting and present value

Why 161 thousand in year 5 is worth less today

5 min live

Keelson's finance team says any money it puts into CFT could instead earn 9 percent a year on something of similar risk. That is the hurdle: the return the money could earn elsewhere. So 161 thousand promised in year 3 is only worth the amount you would have to invest today at 9 percent to have 161 thousand in three years. Shrinking each year's cash that way is discounting, and the shrunk amount is its present value.

LivePresent value in three lines3 min▶

The factor. One unit of cash in year t is worth 1 / (1 + r)^t today, where r is the discount rate. At Keelson's 9 percent the factors for years 0 to 5 are 1.000, 0.917, 0.842, 0.772, 0.708 and 0.650.

The present value. Multiply each year's cash by its factor. Year 3's 161.0 thousand becomes 161.0 / 1.09^3 = 161.0 / 1.295 = 124.3 thousand. The six present values are -360.0k, +41.7k, +86.9k, +124.3k, +114.1k and +104.6k.

The NPV. Add them up: +111.7k. A positive NPV at the hurdle means the project earns more than the money would earn elsewhere at similar risk, by 111.7 thousand in today's money. The undiscounted view, a simple ROI of 75.5%, treats year-5 cash as if it were in hand today, which is why no funder reads it alone.

Self-studyWhy year 5 still counts for 65 percent at 9%2 min▶

People expect discounting to wipe out late years, and at modest rates over short horizons it does not. At 9 percent, 1.09^5 is about 1.539, so year 5's factor is 1 / 1.539 = 0.650: the last year of CFT's benefit still keeps 65 percent of its face value, 104.6 thousand of the 161.0. Discounting compounds, so the bite grows with time and with the rate, but in a five-year case it trims rather than erases.

Two consequences for your own model. First, the years of benefit you assume matter a lot: a sixth year would add another sizeable slice, which is why Analyst session 4 swings the years of benefit and finds it the fourth most powerful input. Second, the late years are also the least certain ones, and discounting is not the place to price that doubt; a range is. Analyst session 5 does that. Raising the rate to cover "it might not happen" mixes two jobs into one number nobody can audit.

Real world

The slide that added the years up. Tom Reyes's first sponsor slide said CFT "returns 75.5% on 360 thousand", from adding the years up. Ines Carvalho sent it back with one line: discount it at our hurdle, then tell me what is left. What was left was an NPV of 111.7 thousand, still a yes, but well short of the headline gain, and the number Ines's team would sign. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 2 · covers the NPV profile and IRR

Slide the rate, watch the case bend

5 min live

NPV is not one number; it is one number per discount rate. Compute it at several rates and plot them, and you get the NPV profile: a line that falls as the rate rises, because every future year shrinks harder. Where the line crosses zero is the internal rate of return, the rate at which the project exactly breaks even in today's money.

LiveReading the profile: the rate at which the case breaks3 min▶

It slopes down. CFT spends first and earns later, so a higher rate punishes it: +210.8k at 3.0 percent, +157.8k at 6, +111.7k at 9, +71.2k at 12, +35.6k at 15. The steeper the line, the more the case depends on the rate, and late-paying projects are always steeper than early-paying ones.

It crosses zero once. At 18.4 percent CFT's present values sum to nothing. That is the IRR. Above it the project destroys value; below it, value is created. Because CFT has one sign change (cash out, then cash in), there is exactly one crossing.

Read the distance, not just the point. Keelson's hurdle sits at 9 percent and the line crosses at 18.4, so the rate would have to roughly double before CFT stopped paying. That distance is what makes the discount rate the least worrying input in CFT's model, as Analyst session 4's tornado will show.

Self-studyThe IRR is the discount rate's switching value2 min▶

A switching value is the value an input would have to reach for the case to stop adding value. Do that for the discount rate and you get the IRR: 18.4 percent, the rate at which NPV is exactly zero. So the IRR is not a separate kind of truth about a project; it is one switching value among many, the one for the rate. Analyst session 4 computes the switching value for every input and finds the discount rate needs to rise by 104.7 percent from its base to break the case, which is the same fact as the IRR seen from the other side.

Two cautions carry over. A profile that crosses zero more than once, which happens when the cash flow changes sign more than once, has more than one IRR, and Analyst session 3 builds one. And when two projects compete, their profiles can cross each other, so the one with the higher IRR is not always the one with the higher NPV at your hurdle. Aswath Damodaran makes both points; Analyst session 3 works them through.

Real world

"What if our cost of money goes up?" At the funding meeting Tom Reyes asked what happens to CFT if Keelson's borrowing gets dearer. Maya Okafor did not rerun the model; she pointed at the profile. At 12 percent the case still shows +71.2k, at 15 percent +35.6k, and it only breaks at 18.4. The board stopped arguing about the rate and started asking about the saving per claim, which was the right question. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 3 · covers which rate to use

Social rate, company hurdle, project hurdle

5 min live

The NPV is only as meaningful as the rate behind it, and there is no single correct rate, only a rate that matches the question being asked. A government asking whether society gains from spending today uses one kind of rate. A company asking whether this project beats what its money could earn elsewhere uses another. And the honest company rate depends on how risky this particular project is, not on the firm's average.

LiveSocial rate, company hurdle, project hurdle3 min▶

The social rate. HM Treasury, The Green Book (2026) sets the social time preference rate for UK public appraisal at 3.00 percent in real terms for years 1 to 30, 2.50 percent for years 31 to 75 and 2.25 percent from year 76 (para 6.54). Its supplementary Discounting guidance of October 2026 builds it from a 2 percent risk-free rate plus a 1 percent systematic risk premium. It answers a public question: how much society prefers value now over value later.

The company hurdle. Keelson's 9 percent is set by its finance team, in nominal terms, as the return its money must beat. It is a constructed policy number on these pages, not an estimate of Keelson's cost of capital from market data. It answers a private question: does this project beat what the money would earn elsewhere?

The project hurdle. Aswath Damodaran's rule is that the hurdle should reflect the risk of the project, not of the firm: a riskier project needs a higher rate. A firm that uses one rate for everything ends up over-investing in its risky projects, which look better than they are, and under-investing in its safe ones, which look worse. A CFT built on proven rules and a few years of claims history may well deserve a different rate from an untested generative model, even inside the same insurer.

Self-studyReal and nominal, never mixed2 min▶

A nominal figure includes inflation; a real figure has it taken out. Keelson's cash flows are nominal: the 14 saved per claim is in the money of the year it is saved, wage rises and all. So the rate on them must be nominal too. Discount nominal cash at a real rate and you count inflation as growth in the numerator without charging for it in the denominator, which flatters every late year. Discount real cash at a nominal rate and you punish the project twice for inflation.

The Green Book's 3.0 percent is a real rate because public appraisal works in real prices. Putting it on Keelson's nominal cash commits the first error, on top of asking the wrong question. The rule fits on a sticky note: nominal rate on nominal cash, real rate on real cash, and write which one you used on the front page of the case. Estimating a company's cost of capital from market data (beta, CAPM) is a separate skill and is not covered here; for valuing a whole company rather than one project, see Learn Value Investing, session 4 ↗.

Real world

The Treasury changed its rate in October 2026. HM Treasury updated the discounting in The Green Book (2026) on 28 October 2026: the social time preference rate for years 1 to 30 moved from 3.5 percent to 3.00 percent real, with the new supplementary Discounting guidance setting out its make-up. Every UK public case appraised after that date discounts its future a little less, which favours long-lived investments. The change tells you something general: a discount rate is a published policy choice that can move, so a case should name its rate, its source and its date.

Real world

"That rate is for hospitals and roads." A vendor's slide deck for Keelson discounted CFT at 3.0 percent, "the government rate", and showed an NPV near double the finance team's. Ines Carvalho rejected it in two sentences: that is a real social rate for public spending, and Keelson is a private insurer with nominal cash and shareholders who could put the money elsewhere. Keelson's case stays at its 9 percent nominal hurdle, with a note on whether CFT's risk is close to the firm's average. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Homework

Before Analyst session 3

30 min
  1. Get the rate in writing. Ask finance for the hurdle your company uses, whether it is nominal or real, and when it was last changed. Write all three on the front of your case.
  2. Ask about project risk. Does your company use one rate for every project, or adjust it for risk? If one rate, write one sentence on whether your project is riskier or safer than the average.
  3. Draw your profile. Compute your NPV at four rates, including your hurdle, and sketch the line. Mark where it crosses zero.
  4. Find one mixed-units error. Look at one past case or vendor proposal in your organisation and check whether the rate and the cash were in the same terms. Note what you found.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why discount future cash at all?

The discount rate is the return the money gives up by waiting. At 9 percent, 161 thousand in year 3 is worth 124.3 thousand today.

2 · The Green Book (2026) social rate is 3.0 percent real; Keelson's hurdle is 9 percent nominal. Which belongs on Keelson's CFT case, and why?

The social rate answers a public question in real terms. Keelson's question is private and its cash is nominal, so the rate must be too.

3 · On CFT's NPV profile, what is the IRR?

The IRR is the discount rate at which NPV is exactly zero, which makes it the switching value for the rate.
