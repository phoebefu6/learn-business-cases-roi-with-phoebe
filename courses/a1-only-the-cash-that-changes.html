Only the cash that changes

A business case model is not a budget. A budget lists everything the company will spend; a case counts only the cash that is different because the project happens. Get that rule right and the rest of the track is arithmetic. Get it wrong and no discount rate or simulation can rescue the answer. Tonight: building the incremental cash flow for Keelson Mutual's claims fast-track triage, the costs that stay out, the ones that go in, and the shared cost that decides whether this project is worth doing at all. Keelson is constructed; the arithmetic runs in your browser.

Part 1 · covers incremental cash flows

Cash with the project, minus cash without it

5 min live

Every line in the model answers one question: is this cash different because we do the project? Cash, not accounting profit, because a business can only reinvest money it actually has. Different, not total, because the case is a comparison with the counterfactual from Leader session 1. For CFT the difference is small enough to write as one formula.

LiveThe one test every line must pass3 min▶

Would this cash be different if the answer were no? If yes, it goes in, at the amount by which it differs. If no, it stays out, however large it is. The adjusters' salaries are Keelson's biggest claims cost, and they are not in the CFT model; only the handling cost avoided on fast-tracked claims is, because that is the part that changes.

Cash, in the year it moves. A licence paid upfront is year-0 cash even if accounting spreads it over three years. Depreciation is not cash; it only matters through tax, which these pages leave out by keeping everything before tax.

Same units everywhere. Keelson's model is in thousands, nominal (with inflation in it), before tax. Analyst session 2 shows why the discount rate must be in the same units: a nominal rate on nominal cash.

Self-studyWhy a dis-benefit is a line, not a footnote2 min▶

Leakage is the overpayment on claims that skip the full review: the adjuster who would have spotted an inflated repair estimate never sees it. Keelson's claims team estimates 4 per fast-tracked claim against a saving of 14, so the net value of each claim is 10, not 14. Leave the leakage out and year 1 would read 161.7 thousand of benefit instead of 115.5, and the case would be wrong by about 40 percent before anyone argued about anything. Analyst session 4 shows that leakage is the third most powerful input in the whole model. A side effect that lands in another team's budget is still cash the company loses.

Real world

The salary line that was not. Keelson's first draft counted the full cost of the four adjusters who handle simple claims as the saving, because "the model will do their work". Claims operations had no plan to let anyone go; the adjusters would move to complex claims, where Keelson was short of staff. The cash that changes is the handling cost avoided per fast-tracked claim (overtime, outsourced reviews, hours released to the backlog), which the claims team priced at 14 per claim. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 2 · covers sunk, opportunity and allocated costs

What stays out, and what sneaks in

5 min live

Three kinds of cost break the test in opposite directions. Money already spent feels like it belongs in the case and does not. Staff and assets the company already has feel free and are not. Overhead allocated by a formula feels like a cost of the project and usually is not. Each one has a clean rule.

LiveSunk costs, both ways round3 min▶

Adding it in. Keelson has spent 45 thousand on discovery. Putting it in year 0 makes CFT look worse (NPV 66.7 thousand instead of 111.7) and could kill a good project for a reason no decision can change. Toggle it in the build-along.

Arguing from it. The more common error runs the other way: "we have already spent 45 thousand, so we must finish". The spent money is gone either way. The only question is whether the cash still to come is worth the cash still to spend. That makes the sunk-cost rule the most useful sentence in a review meeting about a project that is going badly.

Self-studyOpportunity cost when the team is already paid2 min▶

Keelson's data team had 24 team-months for the year, and CFT uses 8 of them. Those salaries are paid whether CFT happens or not, so a narrow cash view says they are free. They are not free if the same 8 months would otherwise deliver another bet with its own value; in Data Strategy, session 3, the portfolio was chosen precisely because capacity was the binding limit. The clean ways to handle it: charge the team at the value of the next-best use of its time, or appraise the portfolio together so the trade is explicit. Pretending capacity is free is how a data team ends up with twelve approved projects and time for four. The constructed CFT model on these pages does not charge for team time; say so on your own case if yours does not either.

Self-studyOverhead: the incremental rule, and its honest limit2 min▶

Aswath Damodaran's rule for allocated costs is that only the incremental part belongs in a project's analysis: a share of head-office cost charged by formula would be paid whether or not the project goes ahead. He adds a caution worth keeping: the company still has to pay for its headquarters, and if every project ignores overhead, nothing ever covers it. His suggestion is that a fixed charge for shared costs can be reasonable. So the honest practice is to run the case on incremental costs, then show what it looks like with the company's standard overhead charge, and let the funder see both.

Part 3 · covers shared costs and dependencies

The cost that belongs to someone else, or doesn't

5 min live

CFT cannot work until the claims data quality fix (CDQ) is live, because fast-track rules read the intake record CDQ cleans up. CDQ costs 260 thousand. Whether that belongs in CFT's case is the biggest single modelling decision on the page: it moves the NPV from plus 111.7 thousand to minus 148.3 thousand. The answer comes from the same test, asked about CDQ.

LiveThree honest answers to a shared cost3 min▶

It happens anyway: leave it out. At Keelson, CDQ was selected on its own merits as a foundation for several bets and is funded centrally. It is in CDQ's case. Charging it again to CFT would count the same 260 thousand twice across the portfolio.

It happens only because of this project: put it in. If CDQ existed only to make CFT possible, it is CFT's cost, and CFT on its own is a project that loses 148.3 thousand. That is not a reason to give up; it is a reason to appraise the pair together, and to ask whether a cheaper slice of CDQ would do.

It is brought forward: charge the timing. A middle case: CDQ would happen in two years anyway, and CFT makes it happen now. Then CFT carries the cost of doing it early, roughly the difference in present value between paying now and paying later, not the whole 260 thousand. Analyst session 2 gives you the tool to compute that.

Self-studyWhy "allocate it fairly" is not an answer2 min▶

Splitting CDQ's cost across the three bets that use it (a third each, or by usage) is good management accounting, and it is the wrong question for a single go or no-go decision. An allocation tells you how to share a cost that is already incurred; a case asks what changes if this one project says yes. The two can coexist: the portfolio view in Data Strategy, session 3 ↗ carries the shared foundation, and each bet's case carries only what is different because of that bet.

Real world

Two answers to one question. Keelson's head of claims argued CDQ should be charged to CFT, "because CFT is the only reason I care about data quality". The CDO pointed to the board paper that funded CDQ for regulatory reporting and fraud triage as well. The fact settled it: CDQ was going ahead without CFT, so CFT's case leaves it out. Had the board paper said otherwise, the same test would have put all 260 thousand in. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Homework

Before Analyst session 2

30 min
  1. Find the dis-benefit. For your own project, name one cost it causes in another team's budget, and estimate it per unit, however roughly.
  2. Settle one shared cost. Ask the owner of a shared platform or data fix whether it would happen without your project. Write the answer and who gave it.
  3. Price the team time. How many team-months does your project take, and what would that team deliver otherwise?
  4. Ask finance one question. Does your company appraise cases before or after tax, and in nominal or real terms? You need both answers next session.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Keelson has spent 45 thousand on discovery for CFT. Where does it go in the case?

The case compares what changes from here. Money already spent is the same whatever the answer is, so it cannot be a reason for or against.

2 · CDQ, a 260 thousand data fix CFT depends on, was approved for its own reasons and is funded centrally. What does CFT's case do with it?

The cost is not incremental to CFT, so it stays in CDQ's case. The dependency still matters: if CDQ slips, CFT's benefits start later.

3 · Why is leakage a line in the model and not a footnote?

A dis-benefit is cash that moves because of the yes. At Keelson it cuts each claim's value from 14 to 10.
