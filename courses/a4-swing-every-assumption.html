Part 1 · covers one-at-a-time sensitivity and the input ranges

A low, a base and a high for every input

5 min live

One-at-a-time sensitivity is the simplest honest test a model can run: take one input to its low, recompute, take it to its high, recompute, put it back, and do the same for the next. The other eight stay pinned at base, so whatever moves the NPV is that input alone. The test is only as good as the ranges, so they come first, and they come from the people who own each number.

LiveWho owns each range3 min▶

The analyst owns the method, not the numbers. A range written by the model builder alone is a guess with error bars drawn on. Each of Keelson's nine ranges was written down by the person who would be blamed if it turned out wrong: the claims operations lead for volumes, saving and leakage, Maya's team for build and run, Tom for how fast adjusters adopt the triage, Ines for the hurdle.

Agreed before anyone sees a result. Once an owner has seen the tornado, every range drifts toward the answer they would like. So the ranges are signed off first, in a short meeting with a table like the one above and no NPV on the screen. If an owner later wants to move a bound, that is allowed, with a reason written next to it.

Low and high mean plausible, not possible. Ask for the value they would be surprised to fall below, and the one they would be surprised to exceed. Not the catastrophe and not the dream; those belong in a scenario, which Part 3 separates out.

Self-studyWhy the ranges are not symmetric2 min▶

Look at the build cost: 30 below base, 100 above. That is deliberate. Technology projects overrun far more often and far further than they underrun, so a range centred on the plan quietly assumes the plan is unbiased. Keelson's delivery lead skewed it up from the team's own past builds, which is the order HM Treasury's Green Book (2026) recommends: the organisation's own historical forecast errors first, generic values only without them.

The same reasoning skews leakage up (1.5 below base, 2 above: nobody yet knows how many inflated claims will slip through) and caps the saving close to base (only 2 above, 3 below), because the claims team already priced the handling cost and has little reason to expect a windfall. A symmetric range is a claim too: it says the error could fall either way with equal force. Write down why, whichever shape you choose.

Real world

The range meeting with no results on the screen. Maya booked a short meeting with the claims operations lead, her delivery lead and Ines's analyst, with one rule: the model stays closed. The claims lead's first answer for the saving per claim was "14, give or take". Asked what would surprise her on the downside, she said 11, because overtime rates were already falling; on the upside, 16 at most. The delivery lead's high for the build, 460, came from the team's own recent platform projects, which had run late. Everyone initialled the table before the tornado was run. Part 2 · covers the tornado

Nine swings, sorted, around one line

5 min live

Run the slider from Part 1 for all nine inputs and you get nine pairs of NPVs. Draw each pair as a horizontal bar around the base case, sort the longest to the top, and the picture looks like a funnel cloud: a tornado diagram. It answers one question at a glance: which assumption is doing the work? For CFT the answer is uncomfortable for the data team, because the top three bars are not data-team numbers at all.

LiveReading a tornado in thirty seconds3 min▶

Read the top three, then stop. The bars are sorted by swing, so the first three tell you where the next hour of evidence-gathering should go. For CFT: saving per fast-tracked claim (a swing of 372.0), share fast-tracked (270.5), leakage per claim (260.4). All three are per-claim economics, and all three are owned by the claims operations lead. The data team's own numbers, build and run, sit further down.

Then look at the zero line. A bar that crosses NPV 0 is an input that can sink the case on its own without leaving its agreed range. Four do: saving, share, leakage and run cost (+150.6k to -5.0k). Build cost does not, even at its high of 460: the case still reads +11.7k there.

Then look at the bottom. The discount rate is the shortest bar: from 7 to 11 percent it moves the NPV only between +141.7k and +84.1k. That is the input finance meetings spend most time on, and for CFT it matters least. The direction of each bar matters too: leakage, run cost, build and the rate all lower the NPV as they rise, so their low end sits on the right.

Self-studyOne at a time hides interactions2 min▶

Each bar holds the other eight inputs at base, so the tornado cannot see what happens when two move together. In CFT they often would: the value of the fast track is claims x share x adoption x (saving - leakage), a product, not a sum. If the share and the saving both land low, the loss is larger than the two bars suggest added up, because each one shrinks the other's base. The same product means the two can also cancel.

So the tornado is a ranking tool, not a range. It tells you which inputs deserve better evidence; it does not tell you how wide the answer really is. Analyst session 5 draws all nine inputs together, and Analyst session 6's bench runs whole combinations side by side, including the sponsor's case where every input moves to its friendliest end at once.

Real world

Most of the meeting on the wrong bar. At the first review, Ines's analyst and Maya's team argued for most of the meeting about whether 9 percent was the right hurdle for CFT. When the tornado went on the screen, the discount rate was the shortest bar of the nine, and the saving per claim was the longest. Tom moved the next action to the claims operations lead: a sample of last quarter's simple claims, timed, to pin down the handling cost the fast track would really avoid. Part 3 · covers switching values and the optimism uplift

How far each input can move before the case breaks

5 min live

The tornado ranks inputs by how much they move the NPV inside their ranges. A switching value asks the sharper question: how far would this input have to move, with everything else at base, for the NPV to reach zero? HM Treasury's Green Book (2026) defines it as the value a key variable would need to change to for the option to stop being value for money, and requires it, at a minimum, for the preferred option (paras 6.101-6.104). It turns a chart into a sentence.

LiveThe switching value as a sentence3 min▶

The shape of the sentence. "The case breaks if [input] moves [percent] from [base] to [switching value]." For Keelson's top risk: the case breaks if the saving per claim falls 10.7 percent, from 14 to 12.50. A board can hold that, check it against evidence, and give it to an owner to watch.

Check one by hand. NPV is a straight line in the saving: the tornado says it moves from -111.5k at 11 to +260.5k at 16, which is 372.0 over 5, or 74.4 per unit of saving. To lose the base case's 111.7, the saving must fall 111.7 / 74.4 = 1.50, from 14 to 12.50. Build cost is simpler still: every extra thousand spent in year 0 comes straight off the NPV, so the case breaks at 360 + 111.7 = 471.7, which is 31.0 percent over base.

Two switching values say something extra. The discount rate's switching value, 18.4 percent, is the IRR from Analyst session 3: the rate at which NPV is zero is the definition of IRR. And years of benefit has no exact break point because it moves in whole years: at 3 years the NPV is below zero, a 40.0 percent cut.

Self-studyThe optimism uplift, done honestly2 min▶

The Green Book (2026) says appraisers must account for optimism bias with explicit adjustments at the outset: costs and durations raised, benefits lowered (para 6.78). Its order of evidence is the organisation's own historical forecast errors first (6.79), and generic supplementary values only where those do not exist (6.80).

HM Treasury's supplementary guidance on optimism bias draws its generic values from Mott MacDonald's 2002 review of large public procurement in the UK. For equipment and development projects, which include software and systems development, the capital cost upper bound is 200 percent and the lower bound 10 percent. The upper bound is the average historic optimism at outline business case stage, a starting point to be reduced as the causes are mitigated; the lower bound is the target by contract award. The evidence is from 2002 data on public projects, so treat it as a ceiling to argue down from, not a measurement of your project. The same guidance offers no sound bounds for running costs or benefit shortfall, and points to sensitivity and switching values for those instead.

Keelson applies a modest 25 percent uplift to the build, above the lower bound and far below the upper, because its delivery lead has the team's own history. The NPV falls to +21.7k: 25 percent of 360 is 90, and 111.7 - 90 = 21.7. At a 20 percent uplift it would be +39.7k. The case survives the uplift, with very little room left.

Self-studyScenarios are stories, sensitivities are tests2 min▶

A sensitivity moves one input and holds the rest; it is a controlled test of how much the answer depends on that input. A scenario moves several inputs together because a story ties them: "a hard winter" raises claim volumes, lowers the share simple enough to fast-track and slows adoption as adjusters are pulled onto backlogs, all at once. Scenarios catch the interactions the tornado cannot see; sensitivities tell you which inputs a scenario must get right.

Use both, and label them. A scenario without a story is just a set of inputs someone picked to reach an answer, which is exactly what the sponsor's case in Analyst session 6 turns out to be. The Green Book (2026) adds a caution worth keeping: putting probabilities on scenarios can introduce spurious accuracy. For weighing options more generally, see Learn Decision Intelligence with Phoebe ↗.

Real world

Why the build range leans up. Bent Flyvbjerg and Alexander Budzier studied 1,471 IT projects and found an average cost overrun of 27 percent; more telling, one in six was a black swan, with an average cost overrun of about 200 percent and a schedule overrun of almost 70 percent (Flyvbjerg and Budzier, "Why Your IT Project May Be Riskier than You Think", Harvard Business Review, September 2011). The sample was mostly public agencies and mostly US-based, so it is a reference class, not a forecast for any one project. It is the reason Keelson's build high sits 100 above base and its low only 30 below, and why an uplift on the build is a correction, not pessimism.

Homework

Before Analyst session 5

30 min
  1. Hold the range meeting. For your own case, get each input's owner to write a low, a base and a high before anyone sees a result. Note who signed which row.
  2. Skew one range on purpose. Pick the input with the most history behind it, usually the build cost, and write down why its range leans the way it does.
  3. Write three sentences. For the three inputs closest to breaking your case, write "the case breaks if ... moves ... percent, from ... to ...", and send them to the owners.
  4. Find your own reference class. Ask your delivery or PMO team for the planned and actual cost of your organisation's last three to five comparable builds. That ratio is your optimism uplift, better than any generic table.

1 · Which bar tops Keelson's tornado for CFT, and what does it tell you?

The saving swings the NPV from -111.5k to +260.5k, the widest of the nine. The discount rate is the shortest bar, and the top three all belong to claims operations, not the data team.

2 · What is a switching value?

That is the Green Book (2026) definition. For CFT's NPV, it is the point where it reaches zero: a saving of 12.50, 10.7 percent below base.

3 · Why are the input ranges agreed with their owners before anyone sees a result?

A range is evidence only if it was written before the answer was known. Afterwards, every bound has a motive.
