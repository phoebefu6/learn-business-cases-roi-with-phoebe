Reading a case like a CFO

A CFO does not read the numbers in a case as a score. She reads each one as the answer to a question, and checks whether it is the question she is actually asking. NPV, IRR, payback and simple ROI can all be correct and still point at different projects. Tonight: which question each number answers, how a high IRR on a small project can mislead a room, and why the hurdle rate underneath them all is a policy someone chose. Keelson Mutual and its people are constructed for teaching.

Part 1 · covers which question each number answers

Four numbers, four different questions

5 min live

Every number in a case is a compressed answer. NPV answers whether the project adds value once the money has been paid for. IRR answers how much room there is above the hurdle. Payback answers how long the money is exposed. Simple ROI answers a question nobody should be asking on its own, because it pretends a pound in year five is worth a pound today. Read the number without its question and you will compare answers to different questions.

LiveThree numbers, three questions3 min▶

NPV: does it add value at our hurdle? In one sentence: every year's cash is shrunk back to today at the hurdle rate, and the costs are subtracted. CFT's NPV is +111.7k at 9%, so after paying Keelson's required return on the 360 thousand, the project still leaves value on top. Positive means yes; that is the whole reading.

IRR: how much margin over the hurdle? In one sentence: the IRR is the discount rate at which the NPV would be exactly zero. CFT's is 18.4% against a 9% hurdle, which tells Ines how far the required return could rise before the case stops adding value. It is a margin of comfort, not a measure of size.

Payback: how long is the money at risk? In one sentence: the years until the cash coming back covers the cash going out. CFT gets its 360 thousand back in 3.3 years, or 3.9 years once the later cash is discounted. That matters to a CFO with a tight cash plan, and says nothing about what happens in years four and five.

Self-studyWhy simple ROI is the number to distrust2 min▶

Simple ROI adds up every year's cash, subtracts the spend and divides by the spend. CFT's cash flows are +45.5k, +103.3k and then +161.0k for three years, which sum to 631.8 thousand; take away the 360 thousand and divide by 360, and you get 75.5%. The arithmetic is right. What it leaves out is time: a thousand in year five counts exactly the same as a thousand next month, and a project that takes ten years to return the same total would show the same ROI. It also has no hurdle, so a positive ROI does not tell you the project beat the cost of the money. Use it as a sanity check on the size of the return, never as the yes-or-no number. Aswath Damodaran makes a related point about payback, which also treats early and late cash the same and ignores everything after the money is recouped; discounted payback is the partial fix he shows. If the cash-versus-profit distinction under these numbers is new, Finance ↗ covers it.

Real world

The page with four headlines. Maya Okafor's second draft listed NPV, IRR, payback and ROI in a row of four equal boxes. Ines circled them all and wrote one question per box in the margin, and only one of the four questions was "should we do this?". The third draft led with the NPV and its hurdle, then gave the other three their questions in small type underneath. The numbers had not changed; the reading had. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 2 · covers how IRR flatters a small project

When a high IRR is the smaller prize

5 min live

Put the quick win next to CFT and the numbers split. The quick win, rules-based triage for 60 thousand over three years, has an IRR of 44.6% and pays back in 1.5 years. CFT has an IRR of 18.4% and pays back in 3.3. IRR and payback both pick the quick win. NPV picks CFT, +111.7k against +41.3k. If Keelson can only fund one, the question is which leaves it richer, and that is NPV's question.

LiveThe question to ask when an IRR looks too good3 min▶

"44.6 percent of what?" The quick win earns 44.6% on 60 thousand for three years. CFT earns 18.4% on 360 thousand for five. A high rate on a small sum for a short time can leave you with less money than a lower rate on a large sum for longer, and here it does: +41.3k against +111.7k at Keelson's hurdle.

"Over how long?" The quick win's cash is +40 thousand a year for three years and then stops. A rate tells you nothing about how many years it applies for.

"Reinvested at what?" Aswath Damodaran points out that IRR quietly assumes each year's cash can be reinvested at the IRR itself, while NPV assumes reinvestment at the hurdle. When the IRR is high, that assumption overstates the return; Keelson has no other projects waiting that earn 44.6%.

Where the answer turns. The two projects' NPV lines cross at 15.4%. Below that rate CFT adds more value; above it, the quick win does. Keelson's hurdle is 9%, well below the crossover, so CFT is the bigger prize. Damodaran's general rule: NPV tends to be larger for large projects and IRR higher for small ones, so when you must choose one of two, the two can rank them differently.

Self-studyWhen two IRRs exist2 min▶

IRR has a second trap that has nothing to do with size. A project whose cash flows change sign once (spend, then receive) has one IRR. A project whose cash flows change sign twice can have two. Analyst session 3 uses a project that costs 100 thousand, returns 235 thousand in year one and then costs 136 thousand to unwind in year two: -100.0k, +235.0k, -136.0k. The engine finds two IRRs, 3.1% and 31.9%. Ask "what is the IRR?" and both are correct answers, which means neither is a useful one. Damodaran's rule is the one to remember: one sign change, one IRR; more than one sign change, possibly more than one. Data and AI projects with a decommissioning cost or a big mid-life rebuild can fall into this. When a case quotes an IRR, check the cash flows change sign only once; if they do not, ask for the NPV at the hurdle instead.

Real world

"44.6 percent of what?" Tom Reyes came to the review leaning towards the quick win: 44.6% looked more than twice as good as 18.4%, and it paid back in half the time. Ines asked four words back. Tom answered that it was 44.6% on 60 thousand for three years, and in saying it out loud he saw the problem. Keelson kept the quick win in the case as the fallback rung, and the debate moved to whether CFT's extra value was worth its extra risk, which is Leader session 3. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 3 · covers the hurdle rate as a policy

The hurdle is a policy, not a fact

5 min live

Every number in Parts 1 and 2 depends on one input the project team does not choose: the hurdle rate. Finance sets it, and it is a policy about how much return the organisation demands for tying up money at a given level of risk. Keelson's 9% is a constructed company hurdle. Change it and CFT's NPV moves a long way without one fact about claims changing.

LiveWho sets the hurdle, and what it should depend on3 min▶

Finance sets it. The hurdle is the return the organisation requires before it will tie up money, and it belongs to the CFO's team, not to the sponsor. A case that picks its own discount rate has picked its own answer. At Keelson, the 9% comes from finance policy.

It should follow the project's risk. Aswath Damodaran's position: the hurdle should reflect the risk of the project, not the average risk of the firm, and riskier projects need a higher rate. Using one firm-wide rate for everything over-invests in risky projects (they are charged too little) and under-invests in safe ones (they are charged too much).

Read the ladder, not the rung. At 12% CFT's NPV is still +71.2k; at 15% it is +35.6k; it only breaks at 18.4%. So the useful sentence for a board is not "NPV is +111.7k" but "CFT adds value at any hurdle below 18.4%". If finance thinks CFT is riskier than Keelson's average work, the case survives a higher rate, and that is worth knowing before the argument starts.

Self-studyWhy a public-sector rate is the wrong benchmark for a company2 min▶

HM Treasury's Green Book (2026) sets a social time preference rate of 3.0% in real terms for years 1 to 30 of a public project. Until 28 October 2026 it was 3.5%. It answers a question about how much society should prefer benefits now to benefits later, for public spending, with inflation stripped out. Keelson's 9% answers a different question: what return does a company require, in money terms that include inflation, to commit its own capital at this level of risk. Discount CFT at 3.0% and the NPV jumps to +210.8k, but nothing about CFT got better; you have answered the public sector's question with the company's cash. Two checks before quoting any rate: does it match the kind of organisation (public or private), and does it match the cash flows (real or nominal)? Keelson's cash flows are nominal, so a nominal hurdle belongs with them. Estimating a company's cost of capital from market data is a separate skill and not covered here; valuing a whole company with a discount rate lives in Value Investing ↗.

Real world

A rate that moved two days ago. On 28 October 2026 HM Treasury updated the Green Book's discounting guidance, and the social rate for the first thirty years fell from 3.5% to 3.0% real. Every public project appraised after that date gets a slightly higher NPV for the same cash flows, with nothing about the projects changed. The rate is a policy, published and revised by the people who own it, and it says so openly. The useful habit for a company case is the same: write down where the hurdle came from and when it was last set.

Homework

Before Leader session 3

30 min
  1. Get your hurdle in writing. Ask finance for the rate, who set it, when it was last reviewed, and whether a riskier project gets a higher one.
  2. Find an IRR that flatters. In a past case or a vendor pitch, find a high IRR or ROI and ask "of what, over how long, reinvested at what?". Write the three answers down.
  3. Find your break point. If your case has an NPV, ask your analyst for the IRR and write the sentence "it adds value at any hurdle below X percent". Your numbers will differ.
  4. Pick the assumption you trust least. One input under your NPV that you would not bet your own money on. Leader session 3 starts from it.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which number answers "does this project add value at our hurdle rate?"

NPV discounts every year's cash at the hurdle and subtracts the cost. Positive means the project adds value after paying for the money. IRR tells you the margin, payback the exposure, ROI neither.

2 · The quick win has an IRR of 44.6%; CFT has 18.4%. At Keelson's 9% hurdle, which adds more value?

IRR is a rate, not a size. The quick win earns a high rate on 60 thousand for three years; CFT a lower rate on 360 thousand for five. Below 15.4%, CFT leaves Keelson richer.

3 · What should a project's hurdle rate depend on?

A firm-wide rate over-invests in risky projects and under-invests in safe ones. The public-sector rate answers a different question, and a sponsor who picks the rate has picked the answer.
