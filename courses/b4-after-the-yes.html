After the yes

The approval is the moment a case stops being a forecast and starts being a promise somebody has to keep. Most cases die quietly here: nobody owns the benefit, nobody measured the starting point, and the review a year later compares after with before and declares victory. Tonight, the three things that keep a case honest after the money moves: an owner and a baseline for every benefit, a register whose alarm levels are the switching values, and stage gates that decide rather than report. Keelson Mutual and its people are constructed for teaching.

Part 1 · covers benefit owners and the baseline

Every benefit needs an owner and a starting point

5 min live

A benefit is not the system going live. Going live is an output; the benefit is the money or harm that changes three steps later, in somebody else's department. HM Treasury's Guidance on Developing Business Cases for Projects and Programmes (2026) asks for a benefits register covering all benefits, monetisable or not, and for each benefit to be assigned an owner. The owner is the person whose work changes, not the team that built the tool. And the starting point is measured before go-live, against the counterfactual, or the review has nothing to compare with.

LiveOne owner per benefit, in the business3 min▶

Why the business, not the data team. The data team controls whether the model is live. It does not control whether adjusters stop reviewing routed claims, whether the saved hours are redeployed or simply absorbed, or whether the handling budget is actually cut. The person who controls those is the claims operations lead, so that is where the saving of 14 per claim gets its owner. A benefit owned by the team that built the tool is a benefit nobody can be held to.

One name, not a committee. HM Treasury's Guidance on Developing Business Cases for Projects and Programmes (2026) says each benefit should be assigned an owner, and the register records who is responsible. A row owned by "operations and data" is owned by neither. The dis-benefit gets an owner too: leakage is watched by claims audit, or by the claims operations lead if audit reports to them.

Ask before the case goes up. The owner signs the row before approval, the same way Leader session 1 had the claims operations lead sign the counterfactual. An owner who first sees the number after go-live will, quite reasonably, disown it.

LiveThe baseline is measured before go-live2 min▶

A baseline is the measured starting value of each register row, taken before anything changes. For Keelson that means handling cost per simple claim, the count of simple claims and an audit sample of overpayment, all measured before CFT routes a single claim. Measured afterwards, the baseline is contaminated by the thing it is meant to judge.

And the baseline is not a flat line. Leader session 1 drew the counterfactual: handling costs were already falling because the claims data quality fix (CDQ) cuts rework. The review compares CFT's results with that falling line, not with last year's level, or CFT gets credit for CDQ's work. The guidance asks the register to record the baseline for each benefit, which is how the falling line gets written down before anyone has a reason to forget it.

Self-studyBenefit maps and profiles in programme practice3 min▶

The Association for Project Management defines benefits management as "the identification, definition, planning, tracking and realisation of benefits". Each verb is a different job: naming the benefit, saying how it is measured, planning when it should appear, tracking it against that plan, and confirming it actually arrived. HM Treasury's guidance puts the same cycle in its own words: benefits management identifies, plans, tracks and verifies benefits, and the register is iterative and attached to the case rather than filed once and forgotten.

In programme-management practice such as MSP (reported), the figure above has names. A benefits map is the chain from what a project delivers to the benefits it is meant to cause, read left to right. A benefit profile is the record behind one benefit: how it is measured, when it is expected, and who owns it. A dis-benefit is a measurable result that someone sees as negative, and it is managed with the same discipline as a benefit rather than left out. The terms are reported here from secondary descriptions and paraphrased; the manual itself is paywalled. Use whichever vocabulary your organisation already speaks; the structure matters more than the labels.

Real world

The owner who said no, then yes. Maya asked the claims operations lead to own the saving per claim. The first answer was no: "I don't control your model." Maya's reply changed it: the lead would not own the model, only the number, and would get the pilot region's data every month to manage it. The lead agreed on one condition, that leakage got its own row with audit watching it, because a saving that came from paying claims nobody checked was not one the lead wanted their name on. Part 2 · covers the register and the dis-benefit

The register, with the switching value as the alarm

5 min live

A register row is five things: what is measured, who owns it, where it started, what the case assumed, and the level at which somebody has to act. Leader session 3 already computed that last one. A switching value is the value at which the case stops paying back; put it in the register and it becomes the alarm. Four rows carry Keelson's case, and one of them is the harm rather than the help.

LiveThe switching value is the alarm3 min▶

Why not alarm on the base case? The base case is a forecast; real results land on both sides of it. Raising the alarm every time the saving per claim comes in at 13.80 instead of 14 trains everyone to ignore alarms. The switching value is different: below 12.50 per claim the case no longer pays back at Keelson's 9% hurdle, and that is a fact worth a meeting.

Four alarms from Leader session 3's table. Saving per claim 12.50 (10.7 percent below base). Share fast-tracked 46.7% (15.0 percent below). Leakage per claim 5.50 (37.5 percent above, because for a dis-benefit the alarm is a rise). Simple claims a year 35,696 (15.0 percent below). Three of the four (saving, share and claim volume) sit within 20 percent of base. The saving and the share can be read within months, so they are reviewed at month 6; claim volume needs a full year to read, so it waits for month 12.

One input at a time. Each switching value holds every other input at base. If the saving and the share both slip a little, the case can break before either alarm sounds. A register that shows two rows drifting the wrong way together is worth a call to the analyst, who can rerun the whole case with the measured values.

LiveWhy the dis-benefit gets its own row2 min▶

Fast-tracking a claim saves the review and loses the check. At Keelson the base case assumes leakage of 4 per fast-tracked claim, against a saving of 14. At steady state that is, by hand: 42,000 simple claims x 55% = 23,100 fast-tracked; 23,100 x (14 - 4) = 231.0k a year; less the 70k run cost = 161.0k, the engine's year-3 cash. Report only the 14 and the benefit per claim looks 40 percent bigger than it is; report only the net 10 and nobody can see which half moved.

The two rows also have different owners and different instruments. The saving shows up in handling cost; leakage only shows up when audit samples routed claims and finds the overpayments. If nobody samples, leakage reads as zero, which is the most dangerous number on the register.

Self-studyMeasuring a benefit properly3 min▶

A register is only as good as its measures. The strongest measure of a benefit is a controlled comparison: some claims routed by CFT, a comparable set handled the old way over the same weeks, and the difference between them. A single pilot region against the rest of the country is a weaker version of the same idea, and it is often the one available. Where a comparison is possible, design it before go-live, because a comparison designed afterwards tends to find what its designer hoped. How to run one well, including how large it needs to be, belongs to Experimentation ↗.

The register rows are lagging measures: they confirm the money months after the work changed. The early signals that a bet is alive (adoption, routing volumes, adjuster feedback) and how a portfolio reports them are taught in Data Strategy, session 5 ↗; this page keeps to the register for one approved case.

Real world

The six-month report. The first draft of the pilot region's six-month report led with how many claims CFT had routed, compared the region's handling cost with last year's, and did not mention leakage. Ines sent it back with the register stapled to it: lead with the saving per claim against the falling baseline, put it next to the 12.50 alarm, and show audit's leakage sample next to the 5.50 alarm, both with the owner's name. The second draft was one table with four rows, and the gate meeting it fed took twenty minutes. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 3 · covers stage gates, evaluation and the option to wait

Gates that decide, and the price of waiting

5 min live

HM Treasury's Green Book (2026) treats appraisal (deciding before) as distinct from monitoring and evaluation (checking after), and asks for an evaluation plan, with its cost, before final approval. It also names real options: building flexibility into a project and exercising it as information emerges, worth most when uncertainty is high and the spend is hard to reverse. A pilot is the simplest real option there is. A stage gate is where the option gets exercised: the register is checked and the next money is released, or not.

LiveA gate is a decision, not a status meeting3 min▶

What a gate is. A planned decision point, set at approval, where three things happen: the register is read against its alarm levels, the owners speak to their rows, and the next tranche of money is released, held or withdrawn. A meeting that ends with "good progress, carry on" and no money decision is a status meeting with a gate's name.

Write the decision rule before the gate. At approval, Keelson wrote: "At the end of the pilot, scale if the saving per claim is at or above 12.50 and leakage at or below 5.50; otherwise stop or redesign." Written in advance, the rule protects everyone from arguing the threshold once the number is in.

The sunk-cost rule applies at every gate. Analyst session 1 left the 45k already spent on discovery out of the case because it does not change between the options. The same holds at gate 3: the 80k pilot is spent whichever way the gate goes. "We have come too far to stop" is the sentence the gate exists to refuse.

LivePricing the pilot: commit now or pilot first2 min▶

Keelson's two paths, at a 55% chance that the saving per claim comes in high (16) rather than low (11). Commit now: +260.5k if high, -111.5k if low, expected +93.1k, worst case -111.5k. Pilot first (one region, a quarter of claims, pilot cost 80k, pilot run 20k, 50k of the build reused): +218.3k if high and Keelson scales, -79.8k if low and Keelson stops, expected +84.1k, worst case -79.8k.

The commit-now expectation checks by hand: 0.55 x 260.5 + 0.45 x (-111.5) = 143.3 - 50.2 = 93.1k. The pilot path's expectation is computed from unrounded values in the engine, so take +84.1k as printed. The difference, the value of waiting (pilot minus commit), is -8.9k: at 55% the pilot costs Keelson about 9k of expected value and buys a worst case 31.7k better (-79.8k against -111.5k). Below a 42.9% chance of a high saving, the pilot wins outright. "Value of waiting" is a general finance term, not a Green Book phrase.

Self-studyWhat the review looks back at3 min▶

The Green Book (2026), chapter 10, separates the appraisal that justified the spend from the monitoring and evaluation that check it, and points to the Magenta Book as the government's evaluation guidance. The Magenta Book distinguishes three questions. A process evaluation asks whether the project was delivered as intended. An impact evaluation asks what difference it made against the counterfactual. A value for money evaluation asks whether the benefits justified the costs. The Green Book asks for the evaluation plan to be built before final approval, with its cost included in the case, and for a later check on whether the costs and benefits actually materialised.

For Keelson the post-investment review at gate 4 answers all three in one table: was CFT built and adopted as planned (process); did the saving per claim, net of leakage, move against the falling CDQ baseline (impact); and did the build and run costs land where the case said (value for money). A review that only asks the first question reports activity. On real options, the Green Book adds a caution worth keeping: probabilities attached to scenarios can introduce "spurious accuracy". The 55% is a judgement, which is why the build-along moves it.

Real world

Ines chooses the pilot anyway. At 55%, committing now has the higher expected value, +93.1k against +84.1k. Ines chose the pilot. Her reasoning to the board was not about the expectation: the claims operations lead could not say with confidence whether the saving would land nearer 16 or 11, Keelson's worst case on commit was -111.5k against -79.8k on the pilot, and a year of measured saving per claim would turn a guess into a register row. Tom pushed for speed; Ines offered him a gate 3 date in writing instead. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Homework

Before your first gate

30 min
  1. Get one owner to sign. Take your register's main benefit row to the person in the business who would own it, and ask them to agree the measure and the alarm level.
  2. Find your baseline source. For each row, name the report or system that will give you the number before go-live, and the date you will pull it.
  3. Write one gate rule in advance. One sentence: at which gate, which rows, which levels, and what happens to the money on each side of them.
  4. Read one past review. Find a post-investment review in your organisation, or the lack of one. Did it compare with the counterfactual, or with last year?
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Who should own the saving-per-claim benefit on Keelson's register?

The owner is the person who controls whether the benefit lands: one name, in the business. The data team owns the output, not the benefit.

2 · What should the alarm level on a register row be?

Results land either side of the base case; alarming on it trains people to ignore alarms. The switching value marks the line where the decision would change, such as 12.50 per claim for Keelson.

3 · In Keelson's widget, when does piloting first beat committing now on expected value?

Below 42.9% the value of waiting turns positive (+2.2k at 40%). Above it, committing has the higher expectation, though the pilot still has the better worst case, which is a separate judgement.
