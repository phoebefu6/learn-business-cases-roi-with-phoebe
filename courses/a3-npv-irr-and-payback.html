NPV, IRR and payback

Put two projects side by side and the four numbers finance asks for will not always agree. Keelson's claims fast-track triage has the bigger NPV; a cheap rules-based quick win has the higher IRR, the faster payback and the better simple ROI. Neither side is lying. Each number answers a different question, and the analyst's job is to know which question the funder is actually asking. Tonight: the ranking clash, the project with two IRRs, what payback cannot see, and how to compare projects that run for different lengths of time. Part 1 · covers the ranking clash on scale and life

Two projects, two winners

5 min live

At 9 percent, CFT (cash flows -360, then +45.5k, +103.3k and +161.0k for three years) has NPV +111.7k, IRR 18.4%, payback 3.3 years and simple ROI 75.5%. The quick win (-60, then +40 for three years) has NPV +41.3k, IRR 44.6%, payback 1.5 years and simple ROI 100.0%. NPV picks CFT. IRR, payback and simple ROI pick the quick win. Draw both NPV profiles on one chart and you can see exactly where they disagree.

LiveWhy the bigger project loses on IRR3 min▶

IRR is a rate; NPV is an amount. The quick win earns 44.6 percent on 60 thousand. CFT earns 18.4 percent on 360 thousand. A high rate on a small sum can be worth less than a lower rate on a large one, and at Keelson's 9 percent hurdle it is: +41.3k against +111.7k. If Ines can fund only one, the company is 70 thousand richer in today's money with CFT.

Scale and life pull the same way. CFT is six times the size and runs five years instead of three. Both push IRR down relative to NPV: the long tail of CFT's cash is discounted hard at high rates, which is why its line falls so steeply. Aswath Damodaran makes the general point: NPV tends to be larger for large-scale projects and IRR higher for small ones, so two mutually exclusive projects can rank differently.

The crossover is the useful number. If Keelson's true cost of capital for this risk is below 15.4 percent, CFT adds more value. Above it, the quick win does. That turns an argument about metrics into a question about the hurdle, which finance can answer.

Self-studyThe reinvestment assumption3 min▶

Damodaran's second reason the two rules diverge sits inside the arithmetic. NPV implicitly assumes that cash a project throws off can be reinvested at the hurdle rate. IRR implicitly assumes it can be reinvested at the IRR itself. For the quick win that means assuming Keelson can keep finding 44.6 percent returns for every 40 thousand that comes back. When IRR is high and the project is long, that assumption overstates the return.

The hurdle rate is the more defensible assumption, because it is what the company says its money is worth elsewhere. That is the core of why NPV is the safer tool for ranking mutually exclusive projects at a known hurdle. Damodaran describes NPV as much simpler to estimate and use once the multiple-IRR problem is in view; that is a judgement about the tools, not a rule to quote as his.

An IRR is still worth reporting. It tells a funder how much room there is between the return and the hurdle: CFT's 18.4 percent is roughly double Keelson's 9 percent, which Analyst session 4 shows is the switching value for the discount rate.

Real world

The slide that said 44.6 percent. The claims operations lead opened the review with one slide: quick win IRR 44.6 percent, CFT 18.4 percent, "the numbers speak for themselves". Ines asked one question back: "if I could only fund one, which leaves us richer?" Maya Okafor showed both NPV profiles on one chart and pointed at 15.4 percent. Nobody at Keelson thought its cost of capital for this work was that high, so the room agreed CFT was the bigger prize, and the quick win was kept as a fallback if CFT's pilot disappoints. Part 2 · covers multiple IRRs

When a project has two IRRs

5 min live

IRR is the rate at which NPV is zero. Most projects cross zero once, so there is one such rate. Some do not. Take a build that costs 100 thousand, earns 235 thousand the next year, then costs 136 thousand to unwind: decommissioning, migrating back, paying to exit a contract. The cash goes minus, plus, minus. It changes sign twice, and the NPV profile cuts zero twice, at 3.1% and 31.9%.

LiveCount the sign changes first3 min▶

The rule. Damodaran states it plainly: one sign change in the cash flows gives one IRR; more than one sign change can give more than one. CFT goes minus, then plus for five years: one change, one IRR, 18.4 percent. The unwind project goes minus, plus, minus: two changes, and the engine finds two IRRs, 3.1% and 31.9%.

Neither is the answer. A spreadsheet's IRR function will return whichever root its starting guess happens to land near, and print it with the same confidence either way. Reporting 31.9 percent would make the project look excellent; 3.1 percent would make it look poor. NPV at the hurdle has no such ambiguity. By hand at 9%: -100 + 235 / 1.09 - 136 / 1.092 = -100 + 215.6 - 114.5 = about +1.1k. It clears the hurdle by a hair, which is the honest summary.

The habit. Before you quote an IRR, read the sign of every year's cash. If it flips more than once, report NPV and say why there is no single IRR.

Self-studyWhere unwind costs show up in data projects2 min▶

Physical projects have obvious end costs: a mine is restored, a plant is decommissioned. Data and AI projects have quieter ones that often land after the benefits have peaked, which is exactly the shape that creates a second sign change.

  • Decommissioning. Retiring a model or a platform still costs money: archiving data under retention rules, shutting down pipelines safely, and keeping an audit trail for decisions the model made.
  • Migrating back. A vendor tool that is dropped leaves its users and data to be moved somewhere else, usually under time pressure.
  • Contract exit. Early-termination fees, minimum-commitment true-ups and the cost of running two systems in parallel during the switch.
  • A planned rebuild. A model that must be rebuilt after a fixed number of years creates a large negative year in the middle of the cash flows.

Leaving these out does not just flatter the NPV. It hides the sign change that would have told you the IRR cannot be trusted. Put the end cost in the year it happens, then count the signs.

Real world

A rule from the textbook, not a corner case. Aswath Damodaran's treatment of IRR in Applied Corporate Finance puts the multiple-IRR problem alongside the scale problem as a core reason the rule can mislead: any project whose cash flows change sign more than once can have more than one IRR, and the same NPV computed at the hurdle rate gives a single, usable answer. Projects with a large cost at the end, such as a clean-up or a forced exit, are the usual source.

Part 3 · covers payback, discounted payback, simple ROI and equivalent annual value

Where payback stops reading

5 min live

Payback counts the years until the cash coming back equals the cash put in. CFT's is 3.3 years; discount the cash at 9 percent first and the discounted payback is 3.9 years. Both are easy to explain, and both stop reading the moment the money is recovered. Simple ROI goes further the other way: it reads every year, and ignores when the cash arrives.

LiveWhat payback is good for: how long the money is at risk3 min▶

The honest use. Payback answers one real question: how long is the money exposed before it comes back? For a sponsor worried that the model might be switched off after a regulatory change, or that the team might be reorganised, 1.5 years against 3.3 years is a genuine difference in risk. It is a good second number.

What it ignores. Damodaran's two objections: payback ignores everything that happens after the investment is recouped, and it weights a pound in year 1 the same as a pound in year 3. CFT's years 4 and 5 are its best years, and payback reads half of year 4 and none of year 5. Discounted payback, which he presents as one simplistic fix, answers the second objection (CFT moves from 3.3 to 3.9 years) and still ignores the tail.

Simple ROI ignores time entirely. It adds every year's cash, subtracts the cost and divides by the cost: CFT 75.5%, quick win 100.0%. A pound in year 5 counts the same as a pound today, and nothing tells you how long the money was tied up. Report it if finance asks, and never rank with it.

Self-studyEquivalent annual value when lives differ4 min▶

CFT runs five years and the quick win three. One fair objection to comparing their NPVs directly: the quick win frees the money and the team two years earlier, and could be followed by something else. Damodaran's answer for projects with different lives is to turn each NPV into an equivalent annuity, the steady amount per year over its own life that has the same present value:

EAV = NPV x r / (1 - (1 + r)-n)

By hand at 9 percent, using the engine's unrounded NPVs (hand arithmetic, not a widget output):

  • CFT, 5 years. 1.09-5 = 0.650, so the divisor is 1 - 0.650 = 0.350. 111.66 x 0.09 = 10.05. 10.05 / 0.350 = 28.71k a year.
  • Quick win, 3 years. 1.09-3 = 0.772, so the divisor is 1 - 0.772 = 0.228. 41.25 x 0.09 = 3.71. 3.71 / 0.228 = 16.30k a year.

CFT still wins, now on a per-year basis that controls for its longer life. The method carries an assumption worth saying out loud: that each project could be repeated on similar terms when it ends. If nothing comparable would follow the quick win, plain NPV is the fairer comparison.

A note on the formula. Damodaran's book states it with a divide, as above. His Spring 2026 NYU Stern slide prints a multiply in its place; work from the book version, and check any template you inherit with one line of arithmetic like the two above.

Real world

The two-year rule. Keelson's finance team had an informal habit of rejecting anything with a payback over two years, a rule left over from a period when cash was tight. On that rule CFT fails at 3.3 years and the quick win passes at 1.5. Ines kept the habit as a risk flag rather than a gate: the case now reports payback next to NPV, and a long payback triggers a question about what could switch the benefit off early, not an automatic no. Homework

Before Analyst session 4

30 min
  1. Find a ranking clash. Look through a past investment paper at your company. Was any option preferred on IRR or payback while another had the larger NPV? Write which one was chosen and which measure decided it.
  2. Count signs on your own case. Add every end-of-life cost you can think of (decommissioning, migrating back, contract exit) to your Analyst session 1 cash flow. How many sign changes does it now have?
  3. Ask finance where the hurdle comes from. Is there one rate for every project, or does it change with risk? Write the answer next to your crossover rate, if you have one.
  4. Compute one EAV. If your two options run for different lengths of time, convert both NPVs to equivalent annual value and see whether the ranking survives.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Keelson can fund only one of CFT and the quick win, and its hurdle is 9 percent. Which number should rank them?

For mutually exclusive projects at a known hurdle, NPV ranks by the amount of value added. IRR favours small projects, payback ignores the tail, and simple ROI ignores time.

2 · A project's cash flows are -100.0k, +235.0k, -136.0k. A spreadsheet reports its IRR as 31.9 percent. What is the right response?

More than one sign change can give more than one IRR, and neither root is the return. NPV at the hurdle gives one answer. Dropping a real cost would make the model wrong.

3 · CFT's payback is 3.3 years. What does that number leave out?

Payback stops reading once the investment is recouped and weights every year's cash the same. Discounted payback fixes the second gap, not the first.
