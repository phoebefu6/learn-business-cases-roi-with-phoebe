Part 1 · covers inputs as distributions

Every input is a triangle, drawn many times

5 min live

A Monte Carlo simulation is less exotic than its name. Each input gets a distribution instead of a single value; the engine picks one value from every distribution, runs the same cash-flow formula you built in Analyst session 1, writes down the NPV, and repeats. Keelson's engine uses a triangle for each input, because a triangle needs only the three numbers an owner can actually give you: a low, a most likely and a high. It draws 5,000 times from a fixed seed, 20261030, so anyone who reruns it gets the same answer to the decimal.

LiveWhy a triangle3 min▶

It asks for what an owner can give. The claims operations lead cannot tell you the standard deviation of the saving per claim. She can tell you the value she expects (14), what would surprise her on the downside (11) and the most it could plausibly be (16). A triangle is exactly those three numbers, and nothing the owner did not say.

It can lean. A normal curve is symmetric. A triangle can put its peak anywhere between its ends, so a build cost of 330, 360, 460 leans up, the way IT builds do, and a saving of 11, 14, 16 leans down. The lean carries real information into the result, as Part 2 shows.

It reproduces. The draws are random in pattern but fixed by the seed. A reviewer who reruns Keelson's model with seed 20261030 gets the same 5,000 NPVs. If your simulation gives a different answer every time someone presses run, the reviewer cannot tell a changed assumption from a changed draw.

The Green Book (2026) names the technique: Monte Carlo analysis is suggested for proposals of high cost or high risk, and decision trees for decisions taken in sequence (HM Treasury, The Green Book (2026), paragraphs 6.73 to 6.76). CFT is not large by Treasury standards, but its sponsor has already asked how sure the team is, which is the same need at a smaller scale.

Self-studyWhat independence assumes3 min▶

Keelson's engine draws every input independently: a high share fast-tracked says nothing about the leakage on the same draw. That is a modelling choice, and it is probably wrong in one important place. If the fast track takes a larger share of claims, it is reaching further into claims that are less simple, and those are the claims where an unchecked overpayment is more likely. Share and leakage probably move together.

What correlation would do. When a benefit and its own dis-benefit rise and fall together, they partly cancel on every draw, so the net value per claim moves less than either input alone and the range narrows. When two costs rise together (a build that overruns and a run cost that overruns, because both depend on the same vendor), the opposite happens and the range widens. Independence is the middle assumption, not a safe one.

What triangles leave out. A triangle stops dead at its low and high. Real outcomes have tails: Flyvbjerg and Budzier found that one IT project in six was a black swan, with an average cost overrun of 200 percent (Flyvbjerg and Budzier, "Why Your IT Project May Be Riskier than You Think", Harvard Business Review, September 2011). No Keelson draw can ever cost more than 460 to build, so the simulation understates the very bad outcomes. Say so on the page that reports the range.

Real world

"Where did these shapes come from?" The first question Ines's analyst asked about the simulation was not about the maths. It was whether the nine triangles were the same ones the owners initialled in the range meeting from Analyst session 4, or new ones the modelling team had tidied up. They were the same, and the table of owners went in the appendix with the seed. A simulation is only as credible as the people who signed its bounds. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 2 · covers reading the range

Four numbers, and the one the base case hides

5 min live

The engine prints Keelson's range as four numbers. P10 is -141.5k, P50 is +20.5k, P90 is +189.9k, and the chance the NPV falls below zero is 44.1%. The mean of the 5,000 draws is +23.1k. Now put the base case next to them: +111.7k, the number everyone has been quoting for four sessions. It sits well to the right of the middle draw. Nothing is broken; the ranges are skewed against the plan.

LiveP10, P50, P90 in plain words3 min▶

P50, +20.5k, is the middle draw. Half the 5,000 NPVs are below it and half above. It is the most honest single number the simulation gives, and for CFT it is much smaller than the base case.

P10, -141.5k, is the bad tenth. One draw in ten is worse than this. It is not the worst case; it is the case a careful funder plans to survive. P90, +189.9k, is the mirror: one draw in ten is better.

44.1% is the chance of losing money at Keelson's 9 percent hurdle, on these ranges, with these inputs drawn independently. Every one of those qualifiers belongs in the sentence when you say it out loud. "There is a 44 percent chance CFT loses money" is a different claim from "on the ranges the owners signed, 44.1 percent of draws fall below zero".

The sentence for Ines. "The base case says +111.7k. Drawing all nine inputs from their agreed ranges, the middle outcome is +20.5k, one draw in ten is worse than -141.5k, and 44.1 percent of draws lose money. The saving per claim is the input to pin down first."

Self-studyWhy the base case is not the middle3 min▶

The base case runs the model once, with every input at its most likely value. The simulation runs it 5,000 times, with each input wandering across its whole triangle. If a triangle is symmetric, its average is its base, and the base case and the simulation agree about that input. If it leans, they do not. The average of a triangle is the sum of its three points divided by three:

Build: (330 + 360 + 460) / 3 = 383.3, above the base of 360. Run: (60 + 70 + 100) / 3 = 76.7, above 70. Leakage: (2.5 + 4 + 6) / 3 = 4.17, above 4. Saving: (11 + 14 + 16) / 3 = 13.67, below 14. Claims, share, adoption, discount rate and years are symmetric, so their averages equal their bases.

Every lean points the same way: costs drift up, the dis-benefit drifts up, the saving drifts down. The base case quietly assumed the most likely value of each, and the most likely value of a leaning triangle is its peak, not its average. Add nine of those small optimisms together and the base case ends up near the good end of the spread. This is the arithmetic form of what the Green Book calls optimism bias, and the reason it asks appraisers to adjust for it explicitly at the outset (HM Treasury, The Green Book (2026), 6.78).

Two more effects sit on top. Saving and leakage multiply the volumes, so a low saving on a low share hurts more than a high saving on a high share helps. And the discount rate and the years of benefit enter the formula non-linearly. Neither changes the lesson: when the ranges lean against the plan, the base case flatters it.

Real world

The Treasury's own warning about precise-looking ranges. In the same paragraphs that recommend Monte Carlo and decision trees, the Green Book cautions that attaching probabilities to scenarios can lend an appraisal a precision it does not have; its phrase is "spurious accuracy" (HM Treasury, The Green Book (2026), 6.75). The practical reading: report the range, name the assumptions under it (triangles, independence, the seed), and never print 44.1 percent where 44 percent, on these ranges, carries the honest message.

Part 3 · covers the option to wait

Commit now, or pay to find out first

5 min live

A 44 percent chance of loss invites an obvious question: can Keelson learn the saving per claim before it builds for everyone? The claims operations lead proposed a pilot: one region for a year, a quarter of the claims, a pilot build of 80k and a pilot run of 20k, with 50k of that build reused if Keelson scales. Keelson scales only if the saving comes in high (16); if it comes in low (11), it stops. The team's judgement is a 55% chance the saving comes in high. The widget prices both paths.

LivePricing the wait3 min▶

Commit now. Keelson builds for everyone today. If the saving comes in at 16, the NPV is +260.5k; at 11, it is -111.5k. Weighted by the team's 55 percent: 0.55 x 260.5 + 0.45 x (-111.5) = 143.3 - 50.2 = +93.1k expected.

Pilot first. Keelson spends on one region for a year, learns the saving, then decides. High: it scales, reuses 50k of the pilot build, and ends at +218.3k, less than committing now because the full benefit starts a year later and the pilot costs money. Low: it stops, and loses only the pilot, -79.8k. The widget prints an expected +84.1k.

The value of waiting is the pilot's expected NPV minus committing now's: -8.9k at 55 percent. At Keelson's own odds, waiting costs a little. Where it turns depends on the two branch differences: the pilot loses 42.2 on the high branch (260.5 - 218.3) and saves 31.7 on the low branch (111.5 - 79.8), so the pilot wins when the chance of high is below 31.7 / (42.2 + 31.7) = 42.9 percent, the break-even the widget prints.

Why the pilot stops. If Keelson scaled after a low result anyway, the pilot path would end at -136.2k, worse than committing now. The option has value only because the team will act on what it learns. "Value of waiting" is a general finance term, not a Green Book one; the Green Book (2026) describes real options analysis as asking whether flexibility can be built in and used later as information emerges, most useful when uncertainty is high or the investment is hard to reverse (HM Treasury, The Green Book (2026), 6.75).

Self-studyWhen a pilot is just a delay3 min▶

It must be able to tell the branches apart. Keelson's pilot is worth something only if a year in one region can distinguish a saving of 11 from a saving of 16. If the region is unusual, or the claims are too few to measure, the pilot ends with "somewhere between 11 and 16" and nobody can act on it. Designing a measurement that can tell the difference is a controlled-test problem; see Learn Experimentation with Phoebe ↗.

It must have a stop rule written before it starts. The -79.8k on the low branch exists only because Keelson stops. A pilot whose sponsor has already announced the full rollout is a delay with an extra cost, and it ends on the -136.2k path. Write the threshold (the saving at which Keelson scales) into the approval paper.

It must be cheap relative to what it protects. A pilot that costs nearly as much as the build saves little on the low branch and loses a year on the high one.

Waiting also caps the downside. At 55 percent the pilot gives up 8.9k of expected value, and in exchange the worst case improves from -111.5k to -79.8k. Whether that trade is worth it is not an arithmetic question; it is a question about how much loss Keelson is willing to carry on one bet, which Ines and Tom answer, not the model. Aswath Damodaran makes the related point that options embedded in a project, to delay, expand or abandon, are side benefits its returns should include (Aswath Damodaran, Applied Corporate Finance and NYU Stern corporate finance notes (Spring 2026)). Full option pricing (binomial lattices, Black-Scholes) is beyond these pages; the two-branch tree is the working version.

Real world

The pilot nobody costed. Tom's first reaction to the 44 percent was "let's pilot it", meaning start small and see. Maya asked two questions before anyone agreed: what saving per claim would make Keelson stop, and who decides. The claims operations lead wrote the rule down: scale only if the pilot region shows the high saving, stop if it shows the low one, and Ines's analyst signs the reading. Then the team priced the pilot properly and found that, at their own 55 percent, it cost a little in expected value and bought a smaller worst case. Ines took the decision to the investment committee as a risk-appetite question, with both numbers on one page. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · CFT's P50 is +20.5k and its base case is +111.7k. What does P50 mean, and why is it lower?

Half the draws fall below P50. The base case uses each input's most likely value, and when the triangles lean, the most likely values add up to an outcome near the good end of the spread.

2 · You hold every input certain and every draw gives +111.7k. What does that prove?

With no uncertainty in, there is no range out. If a simulation still spread with every input fixed, something other than the stated assumptions would be driving it.

3 · When does Keelson's pilot beat committing now on expected value?

At 55 percent the value of waiting is -8.9k; at 40 percent it is +2.2k. The pilot also caps the worst case at -79.8k, but that is a risk-appetite choice, not an expected-value one.
