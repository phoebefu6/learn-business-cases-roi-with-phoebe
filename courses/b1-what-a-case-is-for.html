What a business case is for

Most business cases are written to win. They show one option, one number and every benefit, and the finance team spends its time finding what was left out. A case that gets signed is written to decide: it names the decision, compares real options against what happens if you do nothing, counts the costs a sponsor would rather forget, and says how sure it is. Tonight, the three ideas that make a case a decision document: the counterfactual, the option ladder and the five questions every funder asks. Keelson Mutual and its people are constructed for teaching.

Part 1 · covers the case as a decision document

A case is written to decide, not to win

5 min live

A finance team reads a case looking for what it hides. A sales document hides the alternatives, the costs that land on someone else and the chance it goes wrong. A decision document puts all three on the first page, because the reader will find them anyway, and a case that found them first is a case the reader can trust.

LiveFive things the first page of a case carries3 min▶

The decision. One sentence a funder can answer yes or no: "Release 360 thousand to build claims fast-track triage in the next financial year." A case with no decision sentence is a briefing, and briefings do not get money.

The options. At least business as usual and a cheaper way to get part of the benefit, alongside the proposal. Without them nobody can tell whether the proposal is good or merely possible.

The counterfactual. What happens if you build nothing. Every benefit is the difference from that line, which Part 2 draws.

Costs and dis-benefits. The run cost after go-live and the harm the project does somewhere else. At Keelson the harm is leakage: a fast-tracked claim nobody checked is sometimes overpaid.

The range. Leader session 3 is about this one: the honest headline is a range and the one assumption that would break the case.

Self-studyWhy sponsors write sales documents anyway2 min▶

A sponsor is rewarded for getting approval, not for the accuracy of the forecast, and the forecast is usually checked long after the sponsor has moved on. The incentive pushes every assumption to the optimistic end of its range, one small step at a time, with no single step that looks dishonest. The left page in the figure is not a lie: each of its inputs is inside the range Keelson's own teams gave. It just took the top of every range at once. The defence is structural, not moral: ask for the options, the counterfactual and the range as standard sections, so leaving them out is visible. Leader session 3 covers the evidence on how far optimism moves project estimates.

Real world

The case that came back. Maya's first draft for CFT was one page with a single NPV of 606 thousand. Ines sent it back with three questions in the margin: compared with what, what does it cost to run, and what if the saving per claim is lower? None of the questions was hostile; each was a section the draft did not have. The second draft had the five parts above and a base case of 111.7 thousand, and it was the one her team could check. Constructed Keelson Mutual example; the numbers are printed by the bench in Analyst session 6.

Part 2 · covers the counterfactual

Every benefit is measured against doing nothing

5 min live

The most common error in a benefits claim is comparing after with before. The right comparison is with and without: what happens with the project against what would have happened without it over the same years. When costs were already falling, before-and-after hands the project credit for a trend it did not cause.

LiveWriting the counterfactual in one paragraph3 min▶

Say what continues anyway. At Keelson, the claims data quality fix (CDQ) is already funded and already cutting rework. Handling cost per claim would fall without CFT. That trend belongs to CDQ, so CFT's benefit starts from the falling line, not the flat one.

Say what would get worse. Doing nothing is rarely free. If claim volumes grow and headcount does not, waiting times rise. A counterfactual that holds everything flat hides that cost of doing nothing, which is often the strongest argument a case has.

Say who agrees with it. The counterfactual belongs to the business, not to the data team. Ask the claims operations lead to sign the paragraph before any number goes on top of it; Leader session 4 makes the same person the owner of the benefit.

Self-studyWhy "business as usual" is an option, not a background2 min▶

Public-sector appraisal guidance in the UK, HM Treasury's Green Book (2026 edition), requires business as usual to be carried onto every shortlist, whether or not it meets the objectives, and says plainly that business as usual does not mean doing nothing. It adds that a shortlist should typically include a do-minimum option too, which exposes gold plating, so each proposal is judged by what it adds. Private companies rarely require that formally, and it is worth copying: putting "do nothing" in the table forces somebody to describe it, and describing it is where the counterfactual gets written. The Analyst track turns the same idea into arithmetic: Analyst session 1 counts only the cash that changes between with and without.

Part 3 · covers the option ladder and the five questions

Four options and five questions

5 min live

A case with one option asks the funder to approve. A case with four asks the funder to choose, which is the job they are there to do. The ladder runs from doing nothing to the full proposal, with a cheaper partial option and a staged one in between. Then five questions turn the ladder into a case.

LiveThe four rungs, and why each one is there3 min▶

Business as usual is the benchmark, with an NPV of zero by definition because every other option is measured as the difference from it.

The quick win answers "could we get most of this for less?". At Keelson, simple rules route the most obvious claims for 60 thousand. It returns less (an NPV of 41.3 thousand against 111.7), but it pays back in 1.5 years instead of 3.3. Leader session 2 shows why its internal rate of return, 44.6 percent, makes it look better than it is.

Pilot then scale buys information before the big spend. It gives up some value if the project works and limits the loss if it does not. Leader session 4 and Analyst session 5 put a price on that trade.

The full build is the proposal. It has the highest base case and the worst downside, and a funder who sees both can make a choice about risk instead of a guess about honesty.

LiveFive questions every funder asks2 min▶

HM Treasury's Green Book (2026) organises public-sector business cases around five cases, developed together rather than as five separate documents: strategic, economic, commercial, financial and management. Stripped of the public-sector language they are five questions any CFO asks. Why change at all (the strategic case: what problem, against which goal)? Which option is best value (the economic case: the ladder and its numbers)? Can we buy or build it (the commercial case: vendor, contract, team)? Can we afford it (the financial case: which budget, which year, what it does to cash)? Can we deliver it (the management case: who runs it, how we will know it worked)? A one-line answer to each fits on the first page.

Self-studyWhere the funding question was answered already2 min▶

Which budget pays (central, chargeback, or co-funded with the business) is a funding-model decision, and Keelson made it in Data Strategy, session 5 ↗, together with the early signals that show a bet is alive. This track takes one bet from that portfolio and builds the case for it. If you are choosing between many bets rather than making the case for one, the weighing belongs to Decision Intelligence ↗.

Real world

The rung nobody wanted to write. Maya's team did not want to write the quick-win rung, because it might win. Ines asked for it anyway, and it changed the conversation: with the quick win on the page, the question stopped being "is CFT worth 360 thousand?" and became "is the extra 70 thousand of value worth the extra 300 thousand of spend and risk?", which is a question the board could actually answer. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Build-along

Keelson's one-page case, then yours

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

No code, one page. Work Keelson's skeleton together in steps 1 and 2, then write your own decision in pairs for steps 3 to 5. No numbers yet beyond the ones given; Leader session 2 adds them. Your decision and options will differ from Keelson's, which is the point.

Step 1 · Write the decision in one sentence. Keelson's is given. Check it against two tests: can a funder answer it yes or no, and does it say how much and when?

Step 2 · Write Keelson's counterfactual. Three sentences: what continues anyway, what gets worse if nothing is built, and who signs the paragraph.

★ Step 2 · without CFTcontinues anyway: CDQ keeps cutting rework, so cost per claim falls a little each year gets worse if nothing is built: ______________________________________ signed by: claims operations lead (not the data team)

Step 3 · Your decision. Pick a real investment you want, or one you have seen go to a funder. Write its decision sentence and run the two tests.

★ Step 3 · your decisionRelease ______ to ______________________________ in ______. yes / no answerable? ____ how much and when? ____

Step 4 · Your ladder. Four rungs. The hard one is the quick win: the cheapest thing that would get part of the benefit. If you cannot think of one, ask what you would do with a tenth of the budget.

★ Step 4 · four rungs1 business as usual: _____________________________________ 2 quick win: _____________________________________ 3 pilot first: _____________________________________ 4 full proposal: _____________________________________

Step 5 · Five lines. One line per question. Where you cannot write the line, write who in your organisation could; that name is your first meeting.

★ Step 5 · the five questionswhy change at all: _______________________ best value option: _______________________ buy or build: _______________________ afford it (budget): _______________________ deliver it (owner): _______________________

Keep the page. Leader session 2 puts NPV, IRR and payback on rung 4 and the quick win, and shows which number answers which of the five questions. The decision sentence never changes after tonight; if it does, the case was about something else.

Homework

Before Leader session 2

30 min
  1. Find your hurdle rate. Ask your finance team what discount rate or required return they use to appraise projects, and whether it changes with the project's risk.
  2. Find one past case. Any approved business case in your organisation. Count how many of the five parts from Part 1 it has.
  3. Name your counterfactual signer. The business owner who would have to agree to what happens without your project, and ask them one question about it.
  4. Write the quick win properly. Give your rung 2 a rough cost and a rough share of the benefit. You will compare it with your proposal next session.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Handling cost per claim was already falling 2 percent a year before CFT. What is CFT's benefit?

Benefits are with versus without, not after versus before. The existing trend belongs to whatever caused it.

2 · Why put business as usual on the option ladder at all?

Its NPV is zero by definition. Its job is to make somebody say what happens if nothing is built.

3 · The full build has the highest base-case NPV and the worst downside. What should the case do?

The trade between value and downside is the decision. A case that hides one side makes the choice for the funder.
